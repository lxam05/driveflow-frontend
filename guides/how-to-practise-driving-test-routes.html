How to Practice Driving Test Routes Properly

Updated October 2026 · DriveFlow Guide

Everyone tells learners to "practice the routes" before their RSA test, but very few explain what that actually means. Practicing badly is just driving. Practicing properly means repeating the exact roads an examiner is likely to use, in the conditions you will meet on test day, until the junctions, speed limits, and tricky roundabouts feel automatic.

This guide walks you through how to do that, how often to repeat routes, and how to find real routes near your test centre.

Why practicing local test routes matters

The RSA does not publish fixed routes. Examiners draw from a local road network around each test centre, with variation between tests. That means your examiner will not read from a script, but they will almost certainly take you through the same corridors, estates, and roundabouts other learners encounter.

If you have driven those exact roads ten times, you will know which lane to pick at the roundabout, which speed limit changes at the school, and which estate has the awkward right turn out onto a national road. If you have never driven them, you are reacting cold.

Step 1: Get the real routes used near your centre

Start by opening the Google Maps routes for your test centre. DriveFlow keeps a working set of real routes for every RSA centre in the country. Pick yours from the test centres list and open each route directly in Google Maps on your phone.

Popular ones learners ask about include:

  • Tallaght and Finglas for busy Dublin suburbs
  • Raheny and Dun Laoghaire for coastal and city-edge driving
  • Naas, Navan, and Mullingar for commuter towns with mixed national roads
  • Cork centres like Wilton and Ballincollig
  • Galway options like Carnmore and Clybaun
  • Regional centres like Limerick, Waterford, and Sligo

Step 2: Drive each route at test time, not just when it suits you

A route at 7pm on a Sunday is a completely different drive at 10am on a Tuesday. School zones are empty. Buses are not pulling out. Roundabouts flow freely.

Book your practice sessions for the same time slot as your actual test. If your test is at 11:40am, drive the routes at 11:40am. You will see the real traffic patterns, the parked delivery vans, and the pedestrians the examiner will see.

Step 3: Repeat the same route until it bores you

One lap of a route is sightseeing. Three laps start to teach you something. By the fifth or sixth lap, you are no longer thinking about where to go, so your brain is free to focus on mirrors, observation, and smooth control.

A good target: drive each of your centre's main routes at least three times before test day. If one route has a known fail spot (a tight junction, a confusing roundabout exit, a steep hill start), repeat that section on its own until it feels normal.

Step 4: Practice the manoeuvres on the real streets

Reverse around a corner, the turnabout, and hill starts are not meant to be done in an empty car park. The examiner will pull you into a quiet residential street near the centre. Find those streets on your route and practice there. A reverse on the exact estate road feels very different from one you have only ever done at your instructor's chosen spot.

Step 5: Debrief every drive

After each practice run, spend two minutes going over what went wrong. Did you hesitate at a yield? Clip a kerb? Miss a mirror check before a lane change? Write it down. Next session, that is the thing you fix.

How often should you practice before the test?

If you already drive confidently, two to three focused sessions on local routes in the fortnight before your test is usually enough. If you are still building confidence, start four to six weeks out and aim for one route-focused session a week, on top of your regular driving.

The goal is not to memorise a route. It is to make the local area feel familiar, so that whatever variation the examiner picks, nothing on test day is a surprise.

Common mistakes when practicing routes

  • Driving them only once. One pass teaches you the shape of the route, not the detail.
  • Practicing at the wrong time of day. Quiet evening drives hide the real traffic.
  • Ignoring the manoeuvre streets. The reverse spot matters as much as the junctions.
  • Only driving with your instructor. Sponsor practice between EDT lessons is where most of the real improvement happens.
  • Skipping the fail spots. If a roundabout scares you, that is exactly where you need to go next.

Preparation is the single biggest difference between a learner who passes first time and one who comes back for a retest. Pick your centre, open the routes, and get the local roads under your wheels.
