RSA Driving Test Grade Sheet Explained

Updated 13 August 2026 · DriveFlow Guides

The RSA grade sheet (formally the Driving Test Report Form) is the single page that decides whether you pass or fail your Irish driving test. If you understand how the examiner fills it in, the test stops feeling random and starts feeling like a checklist you can prepare for. This guide breaks down each grade of fault, how many you can afford, and where learners typically lose marks.

Quick summary: You fail if you get 1 Grade 3, 4 Grade 2s in one aspect, or 9 Grade 2s in total. Grade 1 faults are noted but do not fail you.

The three grades of fault

Every fault the examiner marks falls into one of three categories. Only two of them affect your result.

GradeWhat it meansEffect on result
Grade 1Minor fault, noted for feedback onlyDoes not fail you
Grade 2Serious faultFails you at 4 in one aspect or 9 in total
Grade 3Dangerous faultInstant fail

Grade 1: minor faults

These are habits that are not dangerous but are not textbook either. Think of a slightly late mirror check on a quiet estate road or hesitating a fraction too long at a green light. The examiner writes them down so you get feedback, but you could collect a page of them and still pass.

Grade 2: serious faults

A Grade 2 is where marks actually cost you the test. These are faults that could cause a problem if conditions were less forgiving. Examples include:

  • Rolling through a stop line without stopping fully.
  • Not checking a blind spot before pulling out.
  • Stalling in a live junction.
  • Coasting in neutral down a hill.
  • Poor position on a roundabout, especially on multi-lane approaches common around Tallaght and Finglas.

Grade 3: dangerous faults

One Grade 3 and the test is over on the spot, though the examiner will always let you drive back to the centre. A Grade 3 is anything that puts you, the examiner, another road user or a pedestrian at real risk. Emerging into oncoming traffic, driving through a red light, or crossing a continuous white line on a national road would all be marked as Grade 3.

How the sheet is structured

The report form is divided into aspects of driving. Each aspect can pick up its own faults, and this is where the "4 in one aspect" rule matters. Common aspect headings include:

  • Vehicle checks and technical questions
  • Moving off
  • Progress
  • Reverse around a corner
  • Turnabout
  • Roundabouts
  • Traffic lights, signs and road markings
  • Position and observation

If you rack up four Grade 2 marks under "Observation" alone, that is a fail even if the rest of your sheet is clean. This is why examiners at busy urban centres like Raheny, Dun Laoghaire and Wilton tend to mark heavily on mirrors and blind spots. There are simply more chances to slip up.

Where learners lose the most marks

Across Irish test centres, a handful of areas account for most Grade 2 faults:

  1. Observation at junctions. Not looking properly before emerging, or looking without actually seeing.
  2. Roundabouts. Wrong lane, missed signal, or cutting across another driver.
  3. Progress. Driving too slowly for the conditions, hesitating at green lights, or crawling on a national road.
  4. Position. Drifting across lane lines, hugging the kerb, or straddling on approach to a right turn.
  5. Reverse around the corner. Mounting the kerb, swinging wide, or not observing during the manoeuvre.

How the examiner completes the sheet

The examiner is not trying to catch you out. They mark as you drive, using a shorthand of ticks in each grade column against each aspect. At the end of the test they add up whether any rule has been broken (1 Grade 3, 4 Grade 2s in one aspect, or 9 Grade 2s total). If none of those thresholds is hit, you pass. You take home a copy of the sheet either way, which is genuinely useful even if you fail because it shows you exactly what to practice next time.

Preparing with the grade sheet in mind

Once you know the sheet, your practice becomes targeted. Instead of just "driving around", pick a session and focus on one aspect. Spend an hour purely on roundabouts. Spend another on observation at every junction. If you are testing at a specific centre, practice the actual road network the examiner will use. Our test centre route pages map the corridors and junctions that examiners draw from at every RSA centre, from Naas to Mullingar to Tralee.

The RSA publishes the current version of the report form and marking guidelines on rsa.ie. It is worth reading before your test so nothing on the sheet surprises you.
