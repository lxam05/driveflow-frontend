How to Practice Driving Test Routes Without an Instructor

DriveFlow guide · Updated August 2026

Lessons with an instructor are useful, but most of your real progress happens between them. If you want to be genuinely ready for the RSA test, you need hours behind the wheel on the exact roads your examiner uses. The good news: you can cover most of that ground yourself with a qualified sponsor driver, a bit of planning, and a clear idea of what the test looks like at your centre.

This guide walks through how to practice test routes on your own time, safely and productively, without paying for extra lessons you may not need.

1. Know the rules before you go out

As a learner in Ireland, you must be accompanied by a fully licensed driver who has held a full Category B licence for at least two years. Your L plates must be displayed front and back, and you cannot drive on motorways. That accompanying driver is your sponsor. They do not need to be an instructor, but they do need to be calm, patient, and willing to sit through your mistakes without shouting.

You should also have completed your 12 Essential Driver Training (EDT) lessons before applying for the test. Full details are on the RSA website.

2. Find out what your test centre actually looks like

Every centre has its own quirks. Examiners work from a local road network, not one fixed loop, so the goal is to become comfortable with the whole area, not just one route. Before your first practice session, read up on what learners typically face at your centre:

  • Raheny , busy Dublin junctions and pedestrian-heavy roads
  • Tallaght , dual carriageways, roundabouts, and estate turns
  • Finglas , mixed urban and estate driving
  • Naas , national roads and speed-limit changes
  • Carlow, Wexford, Kilkenny , town-centre driving with tight one-way systems
  • Carnmore and Clybaun , Galway centres with rural roads and complex roundabouts

You can see the full list on the routes picker. Pick your centre and study the road patterns before you turn a key.

3. Break the area into three zones

Trying to memorise "the route" is a trap. Instead, split the area around your test centre into three zones and practice them in rotation:

  • Zone A, near the centre: the first 5 minutes of any test. Roundabouts, junctions, and lane discipline as you leave. Get this so smooth it feels automatic.
  • Zone B, the loop: the estates, side roads, and busier routes examiners use for observation, gears, and turnabouts.
  • Zone C, higher speed: the 60, 80, or 100 km/h roads used to test progress, positioning, and safe overtaking behaviour.

Spend one session per zone. Then do a mixed session that stitches them together, roughly the length of a real test (around 40 minutes of driving).

4. Practice the specific skills examiners mark

Driving around aimlessly builds hours, not skill. Each session should have a focus. Rotate through these:

  • Roundabout entries and exits, including lane choice and signalling
  • Reverse around a corner, on a quiet left-hand bend
  • Turnabout (three-point turn) on a wider residential road
  • Hill start on a real gradient your centre uses
  • Parallel parking behind a parked car with space behind it
  • Junctions with poor visibility, using creep-and-peep
  • Mirror, signal, manoeuvre routines on every change of direction
Tip: Ask your sponsor to sit quietly and mark anything you missed on their phone. Review it at the end, not during the drive. Constant chatter from the passenger seat is the biggest killer of concentration.

5. Simulate the real test

Once you have covered the zones a few times, run a full mock test. Start from the actual test centre car park, drive for around 35 to 40 minutes, and include at least two manoeuvres. Your sponsor should give directions only (turn left at the next junction, take the second exit), and stay silent otherwise. This is the closest you can get to the real thing without paying for a pre-test.

6. Drive in test-day conditions

Tests happen in rain, in glare, at rush hour, and in the dark half of the year. Do not only practice in ideal weather. Try to book at least a few sessions:

  • At the exact time of day your test is scheduled
  • In wet conditions, so wipers and demisting are second nature
  • During school run traffic, if your centre is in a residential area

7. Use route data to fill the gaps

Reading about a centre is one thing, driving the roads is another. DriveFlow route pages give you the exact areas examiners draw from at each centre, so you and your sponsor can build a practice plan around real test roads instead of guessing. Pick your centre from the test centres hub and use the route maps as a starting point for each session.

Final thought

You do not need an instructor beside you for every hour of practice. What you need is structured time on the roads your examiner uses, with someone willing to let you make and correct your own mistakes. Plan the sessions, focus each one, and by test day the routes will feel like roads you already know.
