Traffic Light Mistakes on the Driving Test: What Learners Get Wrong

Published 10 August 2026 · DriveFlow

Traffic lights look simple. Red means stop, green means go, amber means slow down. But on the RSA driving test, junctions with lights are one of the most common places learners pick up faults, and sometimes fail outright. It is not usually because you do not know the colours. It is because of what happens in the two seconds around them: your observation, your positioning, and your decision at amber.

This guide walks through the mistakes examiners flag most often at signalised junctions, and how to fix them before test day.

1. Creeping forward on red

You are stopped at a red light. The junction is quiet. Your foot lifts off the brake just a touch and the car rolls forward six inches. The examiner sees it. That is a fault.

Keep the car fully stopped behind the line, footbrake held firm, until the light turns green. If you are on a hill, use the handbrake so you are not fighting creep. This is especially common at busy city junctions around Raheny, Finglas, and Tallaght, where learners get impatient in traffic.

2. Panic-braking on amber

Amber does not mean stop dead. It means stop if you can do so safely. Learners who slam the brakes when the light changes risk being rear-ended, and they will get a fault for harsh braking or poor decision-making.

The rule of thumb: if you are close enough that stopping would need heavy braking, continue through. If you have room to stop smoothly, stop. The decision needs to be made calmly, not in panic.

3. Sailing through late amber (or a fresh red)

The opposite mistake. The light goes amber well before you reach it, you had plenty of room to stop, and you kept going anyway. Examiners watch for this. Going through an amber you should have stopped for is a serious fault. Going through a red is an instant fail.

4. Missing filter arrows and lane discipline

Many Irish junctions have filter lights, right-turn arrows, or lane-specific signals. Learners often sit in the wrong lane, then panic when their light stays red while the lane beside them moves.

Before you reach the junction, read the road markings and overhead signs. If the left lane is straight or left, and you want to turn right, you need the right lane well in advance. Busy centres like Dun Laoghaire, Naas, and Wilton have multi-lane light-controlled junctions where lane choice matters a lot.

5. Blocking yellow box junctions

A yellow box means you do not enter unless your exit is clear. Learners often edge into the box because the light is green, then get stuck when traffic ahead does not move. The examiner marks this as blocking a junction. It is a fault every time.

Only exception: turning right, when you are waiting for oncoming traffic to clear. You are allowed to sit in the box in that case.

6. No observation on green

Green does not mean go automatically. It means go if the way is clear. Learners who launch on green without checking the junction can miss a car running the red, a cyclist filtering across, or a pedestrian still on the crossing.

Quick scan left and right as the light changes. Then move off smoothly. This one habit prevents a lot of near-misses on test day.

7. Stopping over the line

The white stop line at a signalised junction is where you stop. Not two feet past it, not blocking the pedestrian crossing. If you overshoot, do not reverse to correct it. Reversing at a junction is a serious fault. Just hold your position and be more careful at the next light.

8. Stalling on green

Under pressure, learners rush the clutch or forget to select first gear while waiting. The light goes green, they stall, and now there is a queue behind them. One stall is usually a minor fault. Repeated stalls become a pattern the examiner has to grade.

While waiting on red, get first gear ready, biting point found, handbrake ready to release. Move off smoothly the moment it is safe.

9. Ignoring pedestrian signals

At some junctions the traffic light goes green but pedestrians still have the walk signal for a couple of seconds. Watch the crossing before you move. This is a common issue at city-centre test routes.

How to practice traffic lights before your test

Reading about lights is one thing. Getting the timing right takes practice on real junctions in the area you will be tested in. A few tips:

  • Drive your test-centre area at different times of day. Rush-hour light patterns are very different from mid-morning.
  • Practice the amber decision consciously. Say out loud "stop" or "go" as lights change, so your decision-making becomes automatic.
  • Learn the specific junctions on your local test routes. Every centre has a handful of light-controlled junctions examiners use regularly.

Final thought

Traffic lights are not the hardest part of the test, but they are one of the easiest places to lose marks through small habits. Smooth stops, calm decisions on amber, proper lane choice, and a scan before moving off will keep you clear of faults. Combine that with local practice on your test-centre roads and you will handle lights without thinking about them on the day.
