Where Do Driving Test Examiners Take You in Ireland?

Updated 19 October 2026 · DriveFlow

Every learner asks the same question before their test: where exactly is the examiner going to take me? The honest answer is that there is no single fixed route. RSA examiners work from a local road network around each test centre and vary the route from test to test, but the roads they draw from are consistent, and they are learnable.

This guide explains how test routes work in Ireland, what examiners are trying to show you, and how to practice the real roads around your centre before test day.

How RSA examiners choose a route

The RSA does not publish official routes, and examiners do not read from a script. What they do is cover a defined area around the test centre, usually within a 10 to 15 minute drive, that lets them assess every skill on the marking sheet in about 30 to 40 minutes of driving.

To do that fairly, a route needs to include:

  • A mix of 50, 60, 80 and sometimes 100 km/h zones so speed changes can be observed.
  • At least one roundabout, often two or three, including a larger multi-lane one where possible.
  • A busy junction with traffic lights and typically an unmarked crossroads or minor junction.
  • A housing estate for the turnabout or reverse around a corner.
  • A quiet stretch for the hill start and a safe spot for the pull-over and move-off.

Because every centre has a slightly different road layout, each has its own common corridors. Rural centres like Ballina and Clifden lean on national roads and tight village streets. Urban centres like Raheny, Tallaght and Finglas focus on heavy traffic, lane discipline and complex roundabouts. Commuter towns like Naas and Navan mix dual carriageways with older town centres.

What examiners are actually looking for

The destination does not matter. What matters is how you handle each road type the examiner puts you on. Across every centre in Ireland, the same core skills are being marked:

  • Observation at junctions and roundabouts, especially the right-side mirror and blind spot.
  • Correct lane choice on approach, not corrected at the last second.
  • Appropriate progress. Driving too slowly in a 60 or 80 zone is as common a fault as speeding.
  • Smooth, unhurried gear changes with eyes on the road.
  • Clean, controlled manoeuvres in a real estate with parked cars and residents.

If you can show these consistently on any road near your centre, the specific route the examiner picks stops mattering.

Should you practice the actual test routes?

Yes, and no learner should walk into a test having never driven the roads around their centre. You do not need to memorise turn-by-turn directions. What you need is pattern familiarity: knowing which roundabout exits are tight, where the national-road speed limit drops into a 50 zone, which estate the turnabout usually happens in, and where learners typically clip the kerb.

Driving these roads two or three times with your sponsor or instructor before the test does two things. It removes surprise, and it lets you focus on technique instead of navigation on the day.

The short version

Examiners in Ireland take you on a varied local route, not a fixed one. They want to see every core skill on a mix of road types within 30 to 40 minutes of your test centre. The best preparation is simple: practice the real roads around your centre until nothing on them surprises you, then let the examiner pick whichever combination they like on the day.

When you can drive your local area on autopilot, the test becomes about driving well, not about guessing where you are going next.
